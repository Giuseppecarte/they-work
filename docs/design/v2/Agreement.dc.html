THE AGREEMENT
what we are building, in the three languages it has to be true in
TECHNICAL — WHAT THE DEVS BUILD
■
One authored size. Characters and props are drawn at 24 × 34 units. Nothing is redrawn per rung.
■
Integer scaling only. ×4 at the graphics rung, ×1 at sextants. Nearest neighbour, never smoothing — a blurred upscale is worse than fewer pixels.
■
The graphics rung is the design target. A worker gets ~142 × 192 real pixels there. Sextants get ~35 × 36, which carries silhouette, shirt and hair and nothing finer. Half-blocks get a reduced sprite.
■
Parts, not sprites. Six slots assembled at draw time from a thread-id hash, so a face is stable forever and the art stays a few dozen pieces rather than hundreds.
■
Three tones per material plus one shared outline. That is the whole shading system, and it keeps the palette inside Sixel's 256 registers.
DESIGN — WHAT IS NOT NEGOTIABLE
■
Shirt hue is the agent. Orange Claude, blue Codex, grey contractor. Nothing else may use those hues for anything else.
■
Amber means blocked, everywhere, only. Not a theme colour, not an accent, not a highlight.
■
Status lives on the plate, never on the body. The character shows identity; the chip beneath shows state.
■
The room recedes, the people come forward. Walls and floor stay low-contrast. No gradients, no texture competing with faces.
■
Recognition before reading. If you cannot tell two workers apart with the name plates covered, the wardrobe has failed.
POSITIONING — WHAT WE SAY IT IS
■
The line: you started five agents and lost track of them. This puts them in one room where you can see who is working, who is idle, and who is waiting on you.
■
Who it is for: someone running several agent threads at once who is tired of opening each one to find out what happened.
■
The moment it earns its place: a tab dot turns amber and you know, without reading anything, that someone has been sitting on an approval prompt for three minutes.
■
What it is not: not a dashboard of metrics, not a chat client, not a controller. It reads and it draws. It cannot start, stop or answer an agent.
■
Why it looks like this: the charm is the argument. A list of thread states already exists — --once prints one. People will run the office because it is a pleasure to look at.
MOTION — HOW THINGS MOVE
Two frames, not twelve
Every activity is a two-frame loop at roughly 400ms. Typing alternates hands, thinking blinks, idle breathes. Cheap, readable, and it never looks broken at a low frame rate.
Motion means state, never decoration
If something moves, something is happening. An idle worker is nearly still. Nothing animates for atmosphere alone — the eye must be able to trust movement.
The manager is the exception
One figure walks the floor and stops beside whoever is blocked. It is the only sustained movement in the room, which is exactly why it draws the eye to the thing that needs you.
Time drives it, not frames
Animation is a function of the clock, so it is identical on every machine and can be tested by asserting a frame at a fixed timestamp.
STILL OPEN — DECIDE BEFORE BUILDING
1. Do idle workers keep their desks, or drift to a coffee machine? Drifting is charming and costs a walk cycle plus somewhere to drift to.
2. Are per-project office themes worth four times the art to keep good, now that the wardrobe carries most of the variety?
3. Does the half-block rung get a reduced cast, or does it drop to the list view entirely? Nobody has looked at a 17×24 version of this character.
